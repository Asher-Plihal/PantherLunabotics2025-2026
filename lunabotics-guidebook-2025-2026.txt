1. Lunabotics Objectives

The primary objective of Lunabotics is for teams to apply the NASA systems engineering
process to design, develop, build, and test a prototype lunar robot that supports
simulated lunar construction operations. Teams must create a robot capable of
traversing challenging lunar terrain while constructing regolith-based berms—gathering
valuable data on lunar construction techniques in the process.

These berm structures serve critical functions for the Artemis Mission, including blast
and ejecta protection during lunar landings and launches, shading for cryogenic
propellant tank farms, radiation shielding around nuclear power plants, and other
mission-critical applications.

Lunabotics is a two-semester competition that challenges higher education students to
design, build, and test prototype lunar construction robots using NASA's systems
engineering principles. This innovative challenge engages participants in developing
solutions for future off-world construction while providing practical, hands-on learning
experiences that complement academic education.

Selected teams can earn points by successfully completing several key deliverables: the
STEM Industry Plan, the Systems Engineering Paper, the Proof of Life Video, Robot
Data Specifications Report, an optional Presentation and Demonstration, and developing
robots that meet all challenge criteria.

The competition actively seeks participation from diverse educational institutions,
including:
- Vocational, technical, and trade schools
- Community, state, and 2-year colleges
- Colleges and universities (Title IV degree-granting institutions)

Through this multidisciplinary challenge, students gain valuable real-world experience in
computer coding, engineering, manufacturing, metrology, welding, fabricating, installing,
and repairing sheet metal. Participants develop both technical expertise in space
technology development and essential career skills that prepare them for future
professional opportunities.

Challenge Objectives

Teams will submit deliverables for evaluation by NASA subject matter experts.
Successful prototype robots should demonstrate capabilities for constructing off-world
structures that serve critical functions such as:
- Blast and rocket engine plume ejecta protection during spacecraft landings and launches
- Radiation shielding for habitation modules and propellant storage
- Thermal shading for cryogenic propellant storage
- Environmental and radiation protection for power generation facilities
- Other mission-critical infrastructure in extraterrestrial environments

Student Benefits

Participants gain valuable career and technical skills through hands-on experience:
- Creating computer code for autonomous construction operations.
- Engineering and designing robot frames, mobility, sensors and electronic systems.
- Using various types of manufacturing processes to create the robot systems.
- Stress-testing of mechanical components.
- Troubleshooting, prototyping and iterative design improvement.
- Applying professional engineering principles to real-world challenges.


2. Background

NASA is developing foundational technologies and approaches needed to fulfill Artemis
missions on the Moon in preparation for human exploration of Mars. Lunabotics plays a
vital role in this development process. By working in partnership with other government
agencies in the U.S. and abroad, academia, the private sector, and non-profit
institutions, NASA can expand technology and maintenance activities initially designed
for the lunar surface. These collaborative efforts will allow us to explore more of the solar
system in profound new ways.

Lunabotics is a critical asset to NASA, spurring innovation and providing risk reduction
for lunar surface system development. It was established to cultivate new ideas, drive
solutions, and enhance cohesive development of capabilities for productive exploration
of the extreme lunar environment while developing 21st century workforce skills for the
next generation of explorers. Through collaboration across public and private sectors,
NASA and our partners maximize efforts and leverage resources to design and test new
approaches, technologies, and systems in parallel. This strengthens our ability to
achieve successful long-duration missions while increasing access to space, providing
economic benefits to our nation, and creating pathways of discovery for future
generations.

As NASA embarks on revolutionary exploration in our solar system, initiatives like
Lunabotics bridge the technological capabilities needed for sustained human exploration
missions on the Moon that can evolve for other challenging environments in deep space,
including Mars. From a technology development perspective, the Moon serves as an
ideal proving ground for complex Red Planet missions while supporting current and
future lunar science missions. As we enhance our understanding of the challenging lunar
environment, new knowledge from these pursuits advances technology development
that informs our plans for Mars exploration and other celestial bodies, while transferring
applications that can improve everyday life on Earth.

Technologies that enable affordable, advanced manufacturing and autonomous
construction are critical for NASA missions. These same technologies must also support
long-term system maintenance, allowing NASA and its partners to carry out a variety of
missions.

Key technical challenges include excavation of hard regolith and ice materials, as well as
the ability to travel long distances across uneven terrain with obstacles and other
hazards. Both capabilities are essential for establishing sustainable operations in
off-world environments where autonomous systems must function reliably without direct
human intervention.

A concrete example of progress in excavation technology is the NASA Swamp Works
ISRU Pilot Excavator (IPEx). This system is designed to reliably excavate and deliver a
total of 10 metric tons of lunar regolith for pilot-scale in-situ resource utilization
demonstration over the course of 100 meters and 11 days—representing 200 times
more operational capacity than the current state of the art.

NASA's Return on Investment

This competition multiplies NASA's research capacity by evaluating numerous design
approaches simultaneously. By assessing student innovations using the same rigorous
standards applied to internal prototypes, NASA gains exposure to creative solutions for
complex off-world construction challenges.

The challenge helps develop a skilled workforce ready to lead in the emerging space
economy, strengthening America's technological leadership. Technologies developed for
lunar construction often yield innovations applicable to terrestrial construction and
manufacturing, creating broader economic benefits. These advances support space
exploration goals that benefit NASA, America, and humanity's future.

Eligibility

Team Composition Requirements:
- All team members must be currently enrolled students from accredited institutions of
  higher learning, career/technical education programs, trade schools, community
  colleges, or universities located in the United States, its Commonwealths, territories,
  or possessions.
- Students may be registered in either fall or spring semester to qualify.
- Multi-institutional teams are permitted (two schools may collaborate to form one
  challenge team).
- Teams from accredited institutions of higher learning (2-year or 4-year
  colleges/universities) must include a minimum of two (2) undergraduate students.
  There is no restriction on the number of graduate students.
- All students must be 18 years or older prior to attending on-site events.
- Each team must be accompanied by an adult advisor age 21 or older who is
  employed by the registered institution and will remain on-site for the duration of
  the challenge.

Participation Standards:
- All team members must be actively engaged in the robot's design and construction,
  with clearly defined roles to be communicated during Presentation and Demonstration (P&D).
- Students must complete 100% of the project work, including design, robot
  construction, written reports, presentations, and competition preparation.
  Professional assistance is permitted only for training or safety purposes under
  student supervision.
- Excessive re-use of materials from previous Lunabotics Challenge years
  (vehicles, reports, presentations, etc.) will result in disqualification.

Disqualification Criteria:
- Unsportsmanlike conduct by any team member or advisor may result in individual
  or team disqualification.
- NASA reserves the right to exclude individuals or teams for unauthorized behavior, including:
    - Impersonating NASA officials
    - Misusing NASA, sponsoring organization, or commercial logos/trademarks
    - Failing to follow competition rules or staff instructions
    - Falsely claiming NASA affiliation or sponsorship
- Teams not meeting all stated requirements may be disqualified.

PROJECT MANAGEMENT PLAN (PMP) - Due Monday, September 29, 2025

Each team shall submit a complete Project Management Plan (PMP) electronically in one
PDF file. This is an initial plan. As you execute your project, things will change and your
project will evolve, which is okay and expected. In your Systems Engineering Paper you
can discuss the changes to your plan and how your project adapted.

Include your school's name on the PMP.
The maximum length of the plan is 5 pages. If you include a cover page, it will not count
towards the page limit. Any content over 5 pages will not be judged and will not be scored.

Format: The Project Management Plan shall be formatted professionally, organized clearly
so that each required rubric element is easy to find, with correct spelling and grammar,
with text no smaller than size 12-point font in the main body, with text no smaller than
size 9-point font in graphics and tables, and using professional margins.

Scoring Rubric - Project Management Plan

Element: Initial Project Schedule                                    Points: 3 total for 6 elements
  Provide a Gantt Chart or equivalent that shows the project's major
  due dates and events to include at least:
  1. Start Date
  2. Completion Date (after project decommissioning)
  3. Dates for Major Review milestones (minimum):
     - Systems Requirements Review
     - Preliminary Design Review
     - Critical Design Review
     - others as appropriate
  4. Product delivery dates including:
     - Systems Engineering Paper delivery
     - Planned date to submit "Proof of Life"
  5. Critical milestones related to Project Cost Budget
  6. Critical milestones related to Technical Performance Measures
  7. (Optional) Major Systems Engineering activities

  Discuss how you will manage the evolution of the schedule during the life of the project.

Element: Initial Project Cost Budget                                 Points: 3 total for 3 elements
  Provide an estimate of the Total Project Cost, inclusive of all possible costs
  throughout the project life cycle.

  Include:
  1. Breakdown of total project cost for at least:
     a. System Hierarchy cost estimates:
        1. Labor costs, if any
        2. Material costs
     b. Travel costs including UCF and KSC
  Identify critical scheduling milestones for budget items.
  Discuss how you will manage budget evolution.

Element: Project Technical Objectives                                Points: 1 point for 1 element
  List and discuss specific technical criteria your team intends to achieve.

Element: Initial Technical Performance Measures (Technical Budgets) Points: 3 total for 5 elements
  Provide a Table of Technical Performance Measures including:
  1. Identification of TPMs chosen to monitor and manage
  2. Initial Target Value for each TPM
  Provide allocation of each TPM value across elements of the system hierarchy.
  Discuss critical schedule milestones for achieving critical technical performance levels.
  Discuss how you will manage the evolution of TPM budgets.

PMP Tips:
Tip #1 - Make sure your Gantt Chart and tables are readable. If unreadable (less than
9-point text as viewed in the PDF), ensure rubric elements are discussed in the main body.

Tip #2 - Initial Project Schedule: Major Reviews. At least 3 must appear on your schedule:
SRR, PDR, and CDR. If using alternate review names, identify which replaces each.

Tip #3 - Developing any management plan boils down to two things: making decisions and
writing them down. Document decisions at the beginning of the project and share with your
team. Update your plan as you learn more.

Tip #4 - Initial Project Budget: Budget evolution. Set up periodic reviews of your cost budget.
Decide how you will address budget shortfalls and what you will do with budget excesses.

Tip #5 - Initial Technical Performance Measures (TPM): Allocation to System Hierarchy.
At the very start, the hierarchy may only consist of two levels. That early decomposition
allows you to allocate time, money, and TPM values across each sub-element.

SRR Report Requirements:
1. Include your School Name and Team Name.
2. Maximum length: 4 pages (cover page does not count toward limit).
   Any content over 4 pages will not be judged or scored.

Scoring Rubric - Systems Requirements Review Report

Page 1 Content: Mission Analysis                                     Points: 5
  1. Functional Mission Statement for the System [1 point]
  2. All Stakeholders and Customers (internal/external, their roles) [1 point]
  3. System Boundary Sketch (Functional Block Diagram) identifying all top-level
     functional elements and external elements [3 points]

Page 2 Content: Integrated Operational Scenario                      Points: 5
  1. Explain how system elements will be operated to accomplish the mission,
     including all Stakeholders. Scenario begins at formal baseline through
     transport to competition and ending with final disposal of the system. [5 points]

Page 3 Content: Systems Requirements Baselined at the SRR            Points: 5
  1. Table including:
     a. Key requirements (functional and performance) derived from customer
        documentation, stated in proper "shall" language. [2 points]
     b. All additional requirements necessary to execute the Integrated
        Operational Scenario, in proper "shall" language. [2 points]
     c. Planned Verifications for each SRR baselined requirement including:
        i. When verification will occur
        ii. How verification will be executed
        iii. Success criteria for the verification [0.5 point]
  2. Identify requirements related to reliability, maintainability, logistics,
     transportation, human factors/operations, training, and safety. [0.5 point]

Page 4 Content: Systems Requirements Review                          Points: 5 for 10 elements
  1. Explain how the SRR was conducted:
     a. Identify reviewers (internal/external)
     b. How the proposed problem space baseline was presented
     c. How reviewer comments were collected
     d. How responses to reviewer comments were developed and presented
     e. How consensus was achieved
     f. How baseline approval was obtained
  2. Explicit examples of Changes resulting from reviewer comments:
     a. Changes to Schedule
     b. Changes to Cost Budget and Allocations
     c. Changes to TPMs and Allocations
     d. Changes to System Requirements (added, deleted, modified) and verifications

RoboPits

The RoboPits are located in the Astronauts Memorial Foundation's (AMF) Center for
Space Education (CSE) Building, room M6-306. This is where teams will meet with
competitors from across the country and prepare robots before competing in the Artemis
Arena. Robots will be inspected before each competition attempt, and teams are
permitted to repair or modify their robots while the RoboPits are open.

The RoboPits are equipped with emergency eyewash stations and disposal containers
for used aerosol cans, batteries, degreasers, and cleaning wipes. Teams are encouraged
to bring additional LED lighting, floor coverings or mats to facilitate cleaning, power
strips, and a first-aid kit to enhance their workspace.

Teams are responsible for creating team pit identification sign. Signs must be under 6
feet tall and may include LED light strings. KSC Fire/Safety personnel may require
modifications or removal if a sign is deemed unsafe.

RoboPits Check-in Procedures

Spectators must follow the same rules as participants.

Step 1: Check-in and Setup

Upon arrival, the Pit Boss will assign teams pit location and explain the inspection
process, run sign-up procedures, and other competition protocols. Please remain
professional and patient during busy periods.

Contact Information:
Teams must provide two contact phone numbers for emergency communication during
the competition. These numbers will remain confidential and be deleted at the
competition's conclusion.

Pit Specifications, Guidelines & Shared Equipment:
Teams assigned 10' x 10' pit includes two chairs, one table, and two power outlets with
provided power strips. Do not daisy-chain power strips. Keep all team members and
equipment within assigned pit boundaries, ensuring walkways and hallways remain clear.

Vacuums are provided for shared use by all teams. Return vacuums to the designated
area after use and notify the Pit Boss of any safety concerns.

KSC Prototype Development Laboratory (PDL) Bot Shop:
The PDL is staffed by KSC engineering technicians who specialize in designing,
fabricating, and testing prototypes and test equipment. This mobile machine shop is
equipped with grinding, sanding, mini-mill and mini-lathe, band saw, drill press, and
hand tools (welding is not available). Only PDL mechanics are authorized to operate the
equipment. First-come, first-served basis. Protective eyewear required at all times.

Step 2: Clean-up and Check-out

The RoboPit must be kept neat each night with nothing outside the pit boundaries.
Unplug all equipment before leaving. Each team must leave their RoboPit in the same
condition as found and request a final inspection from the Pit Boss.

Industrial Waste Protocol

Teams must comply with Federal and Kennedy Space Center hazardous and controlled
waste program requirements. Coordinate with the RoboPit Chief before disposing of:

Regolith Simulant - Black Point-1 (BP-1)

BP-1 is a crushed lava basalt aggregate with particle size distribution similar to lunar
soil. Important safety considerations:
- BP-1 is alkaline and may cause skin and eye irritation
- Contains a small percentage of crystalline silica, which is a respiratory irritant
- If a person is allergic to talcum powder, they may be allergic to BP-1
- All participants must wear ANSI-approved, UL-Listed, CE EN166 rated, AS/NZS
  certified, or CSA rated PPE when in contact with BP-1
- Respiratory protection is required

ROBOTS AND ROBOTIC OPERATIONS

1. Robots

Lunar bulk regolith construction requires teams to consider several design and operation
factors such as high robot dust tolerance and minimizing dust projection, efficient
communications, minimizing vehicle mass, minimizing energy/power required, and
maximizing autonomy. Each team will have the opportunity to complete two construction
competition runs to demonstrate their design.

Students on the team shall perform 100% of this project (including design, construction,
and operations task components of their vehicle and deliverables, and including performing
or supervising work that is supported by a professional machinist for the purpose of
training or safety). Components are not required to be space qualified for atmospheric,
electromagnetic, thermal, or Lunar environments.

2. Robot Requirements

1. The robot(s) shall be contained within a payload envelope measuring 150 cm length
   x 75 cm width x 75 cm height. The orientation of these dimensions may be chosen
   by the team.
2. The robot may deploy or expand beyond the envelope after the start of each attempt
   but shall not exceed 175 cm in additional height (250 cm above the regolith surface).
3. Multiple robot systems are allowed but the starting dimensions of the whole system
   shall comply with the volumetric dimensions given in this rule.
4. Robots will be inspected for volumetric dimensions in the stowed configuration during
   safety inspection. No modifications permitted during this verification.
5. The robot shall have a maximum mass of 80 kg. Subsystems used to transmit
   commands/data and video to telerobotic operators are counted toward the mass limit.
6. The mass of the navigational aid system, including any beacons or targets not
   attached to the robot, is included in the maximum mining robot mass limit and must
   be self-powered.
7. Equipment not on the robot used to receive data from and send commands to the
   robot for telerobotic operations is excluded from the mass limit.
8. Multiple robot systems are allowed but total mass of the whole system shall comply
   with the mass limit. Lower masses will result in lower mission costs—this competition
   rewards teams that have a lower robot mass.
9. The launch volume dimensions of the robot may be oriented in any way.
10. The team must declare robot orientation to the inspection judge:
    Arrow 1 – length, Arrow 2 – width, Arrow 3 – forward direction of robot.
11. The judges will use Arrow 3 to orient the robot in the randomly selected direction and position.
12. Teams may use honeycomb structures as long as they are strong enough to be safe
    and the edges sealed to prevent dust intrusion.
13. Teams may NOT use: global positioning systems (GPS), rubber pneumatic tires,
    air/foam filled tires, open or closed cell foam, ultrasonic proximity sensors, or hydraulics.
14. Robots shall have a minimum of four (4) designated lifting points, safe for human
    hands and clearly marked. Minimum of one person per 20 kg of mass; minimum
    four people to carry the maximum allowed mass of 80 kg.
15. The robot can separate itself intentionally but all parts must be under the team's
    control at all times. Unintentional breakage will not be counted against the team.
16. The robot can run either by telerobotic (remote control) or autonomous operations.
17. External robot antennas are recommended to reduce potential interference problems.
18. The robot cannot have any touch sensors to sense and avoid obstacles.

3. E-STOP Button

1. The robot shall be equipped with an E-STOP button. An unmodified "Commercial
   Off-The-Shelf" (COTS) red button is required. Failure will result in a safety
   disqualification. Minimum diameter of 40 mm, requiring no additional steps to access.
2. The E-STOP button shall be placed on the highest practical location on the robot.
   There shall be only one E-STOP button per robot; multiple robots each need their own.
3. Disabling the E-STOP button without authorization shall result in a safety disqualification.
4. Only onboard laptop computers may stay powered on if powered by its own, independent
   internal computer battery.
5. OSHA and relevant standards (IEC 60204-1) state that an e-stop must be readily
   accessible to the operator. It should be unobstructed and easily accessible without
   having to reach over, under or around it.
6. Resetting of the e-stop alone shall not resume operation. A second deliberate action
   is needed (e.g., pressing a reset button or pulling the button back up). It cannot
   automatically reset.
7. The E-STOP button shall stop the robot's motion and disable power with one push.
   It shall disconnect the batteries from all controllers and isolate the batteries from the
   rest of the active sub-systems.

4. Power Meters/Data Loggers

1. Power Meters/Data Loggers shall be placed on the highest practical location on the
   robot and be easily visible.
2. The robot shall provide its own onboard power. No facility power will be provided
   during the attempt. The data logger shall be schematically located between the
   battery and kill switch. A 30 point reduction to BCP Energy will be accessed if the
   robot is not wired in accordance with this requirement.
3. The energy consumed shall be recorded with a COTS electronic data logger device.
   Actual energy consumed during each attempt shall be shown to the judges on the
   data logger immediately after the attempt.

5. Battery Protocol

1. Lithium-Ion/Nic-Cad batteries used in robots must be attended while charging.
   Chargers shall be unplugged overnight.
2. Battery containers must be designed for safely storing, charging, and transporting
   lithium-ion batteries, or approved equivalent.
3. Batteries must be stored in upright containers; batteries cannot be in contact with each other.
4. Batteries that have been dropped must be inspected for damage and replaced as needed.
5. Do not store batteries that are hot to the touch after charging.
6. If a battery continues to feel hot after charging, if possible, remove from the building
   and place outside and notify NASA Fire as a non-emergency issue.
7. No one will fault you if you need to call NASA Fire at 321.867.7911.
8. To ensure the robot is usable for an actual mission, it cannot employ any fundamental
   physical processes, gases, fluids, or consumables that would not work in an off-world
   environment.
9. Closed loop pneumatic systems are allowed if the gas is supplied by the robot itself.
10. Examples of allowable components: Sealed Lead-Acid (SLA) or Nickel Metal Hydride
    (NiMH) batteries; composite materials; rubber or plastic parts; actively fan cooled
    electronics; motors with brushes; infrared sensors; inertial measurement units;
    proximity detectors and/or Hall Effect sensors.

6. Robotic Operations

1. The robot cannot be anchored to the regolith simulant prior to the beginning of the demonstration.
2. At the start of the competition run, the mining robot may not occupy any location outside the defined starting position.
3. The robot must operate within the Artemis Arena; not permitted to pass beyond the confines of the outside perimeter or hit the walls.
4. The robot may not use any process that causes the physical or chemical properties of the regolith simulant to be changed.
5. No ordnance, projectile, far-reaching mechanism, etc. may be used.
6. Far-reaching mechanism does not include any deployed or extended component as allowed in the dimensions rule.
7. Beacons or fiducial targets may be attached to the designated arena frame area for navigation purposes only.
8. Only Class I or Class II laser or low powered lasers (< 5mW) are allowed.


SCORING, CONSTRUCTION, NAVIGATION

1. Scoring

The mission of this robot system is to provide a berm that protects astronauts and habitat
modules from plume ejecta from vehicles lifting off and landing on the opposite side of
the berm. For this competition, plume ejecta is presumed to move perpendicular to the
surface and parallel to the short axis of the berm area. Both mass (or volume) of the
berm and the projected area of the berm normal to the plume ejecta provide this protection.

1. UCF & KSC:
   a. The berm construction and bandwidth scores from each run will be added together
      for the final score at each venue (cumulative, not the highest or an average).
      Scores will NOT be added across the venues.
   b. The Caterpillar Autonomy Award will be based on the sum of autonomy scores
      from both UCF runs and runs at KSC.

2. KSC Only:
   Team scores for construction will be normalized to the maximum construction points
   achieved by all teams to a scale between 0 and 25 points for the grand prize.

2. Construction Points

1. Pass All Inspections (Comm/Vehicle) - Required before first competition run.
   Neither inspection is optional; if one cannot be passed, robot will not be permitted
   in the Arena.

2. Construction Berm Productivity – Normalized for Robot Mass (BCP Mass)
   A volumetric scan before and after the run will be performed. Only berm volume
   within the target area (perimeter dimensions 2.2 M x 0.9 M) will be counted.
   No restriction on shape, height, or number of berms.
   Points: cm^3 berm / min / kg robot mass  (specific points: 4.4)

3. Construction Berm Productivity – Normalized for Energy (BCP Energy)
   A volumetric scan before and after the run will be performed. Only berm volume
   within the target berm location will be counted. Energy consumed must be displayed
   by COTS electronic data logger and verified by a judge.
   NOTE: A 30 point penalty will be assessed if your robot energy data logger is not
   wired in accordance to the Robots and Robotic Operations section 4.
   Points: cm^3 berm / min / watthour  (specific points: 1.5)

4. Bandwidth Use
   During each competition attempt, the team will be scored on bandwidth usage:
   Total average bandwidth (TAB) = ((# arena cameras * 0.5) + avg bandwidth in Mbps)
   If TAB is greater than 4 then 0, else, (TAB * -30) + 120 = score.
   
   Example: (1) arena camera used, 0.7 avg bandwidth Mbps
   TAB = (1*0.5) + 0.7 = 1.2
   (1.2 * -30) + 120 = 84 points

5. Dust Tolerant Design - Up to 30 points per attempt:
   a. Drive train and components enclosed/protected: 15 points
   b. Active dust control (brushing, electrostatics, etc.): 5 points
   c. Custom dust sealing features (bellows, seals, etc.): 10 points

6. Dust Free Operation - Up to 30 points per attempt:
   - Driving without dusting up regolith: 5 points
   - Digging without dusting up regolith: 20 points
   - Transferring regolith without dumping on your own robot: 5 points

Berm Size:          2.0m x 0.7m   1.3m x 0.7m
Target Berm Area:   2.2m x 0.9m   1.5m x 0.9m

Note: Only berm volume inside the red box (target area) will count toward the measurement.

4. Construction Protocol

1. Robot will be inspected before each competition attempt. Teams may repair or modify
   robots while RoboPits are open.
2. Teams are responsible for placement and removal of robot onto the arena surface.
   One person per 20 kg; minimum four people for maximum 80 kg robot.
3. Each team is allowed up to 10 minutes to place the construction robot and perform
   required setups, and 5 minutes to remove the robot after the attempt.
4. Robot's starting direction and location will be randomly selected immediately before the attempt.
5. At the start of each attempt, the robot shall not occupy any location outside the defined starting position.
6. Robot shall move from starting area, across obstacle zone, and into excavation zone.
   Robot shall NOT acquire regolith for the berm from inside the starting area, obstacle
   zone, or construction zone. All regolith for berm construction must be acquired from
   the excavation zone.
7. The robot shall not push or move any obstacles in the obstacle zone.
8. Obstacles may only be pushed to the side of the arena in the construction zone.
9. The robot shall avoid the craters in the obstacle zone (shall not fill in any craters).
10. Robot may start excavation operations as soon as any part of it crosses into the excavation zone.
11. Robot may start construction operations as soon as any part of it crosses into the construction zone.
12. Robot shall operate within the arena; no part permitted to pass beyond the outside wall.
13. Robot can separate itself intentionally but all parts must remain under team's control.
14. The robot shall NOT:
    - Be anchored to the arena surface prior to the beginning of each attempt
    - Ram the wall (may result in a safety disqualification for that attempt)
    - Use any aspect of the arena (wall, structure, column, etc.) in attempting any operations
    - Use any process that causes the physical or chemical properties of the regolith simulant to be changed
15. Bulldozing (pushing a pile of dirt/rocks) with a bladed dozer-type rover is NOT
    considered acceptable. All regolith must be carried through the obstacle zone.
16. Obstacles may not be moved into the excavation zone.
17. Robot shall end operations immediately when the power-off command is sent.

4. Navigation Protocol

1. Team must declare robot orientation by length and width to the inspection judge.
   An arrow marking the forward direction is required. Judges will orient the robot in
   the randomly selected direction (north, east, south, or west).
2. Compasses (analog, digital, etc.) are not allowed on the robot.
3. Global Positioning Satellite (GPS) or IMU-enabled GPS devices are not allowed.
4. The mass of the navigational aid system is included in the 80.0 kg maximum mass limit.
5. Target Beacons may be attached to the provided mounting system in the starting area.
6. Target/beacon may be a passive fiducial, or it may send a signal or light beam.
   Only Class I or Class II lasers or low-powered lasers (< 5mW) are allowed.
7. Inertial measurement units (IMU) are allowed. Teams must explain how the compass
   feature will be switched off or subtracted.
8. During each competition attempt, the robot is limited to autonomous and telerobotic operations only.

5. Autonomy Rules

1. Telemetry to monitor health of construction robot is allowed during the autonomous
   period. Teams will need to remain "hands free" during any attempts at autonomy points.
2. Teams shall not touch the controls during the autonomous period. Orientation data
   cannot be transmitted to the construction robot in the autonomous period.
3. The walls shall NOT be used for the purposes of mapping autonomous navigation and
   collision avoidance (there are no walls on off-world locations).
4. Team must explain to the inspection judges how their autonomous systems work and
   prove that autonomy sensors do not use the walls.
5. Teams are allowed to interact with an interface that allows different pieces of telemetry
   data to be viewed as long as there is no real-time or other interaction to control the robot.
6. Teams are not permitted to update or alter the autonomy program to account/detect
   or upload information about obstacle locations.
7. Failure to divulge the method of autonomy sensing shall result in disqualification.


MISSION CONTROL CENTER AND AUTONOMOUS OPERATIONS

Mission Control Center (MCC):

Teams will control or autonomously operate their robots from the Mission Control Center
(MCC) to simulate operations of a Lunar In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) construction
mission. Lunabotics Mission Control Judges (MCJs) will supervise team activities in the
MCC. A Mission Control Director (MCD) will serve as the Lead Judge for the MCC.

1. General Guidelines

1. Each team will be allowed a maximum of 4 team members in the MCC. All members
   must enter the MCC together when authorized by the MCJ.
2. Faculty/Advisors are not permitted in the MCC at any time.
3. Teams are responsible for ensuring they enter the MCC with all mission-critical
   components and spares they require. Once in the MCC, team members will not be
   permitted to retrieve forgotten items.
4. Teams may only bring electronic devices required for robot operations into the MCC.
   Extraneous laptops, cell phones, smart devices, etc., are prohibited.
5. Teams that have entered the MCC are not allowed any external communications until
   the completion of their run. The one exception is communication with their Artemis
   Arena teammates during the setup period, using equipment provided by Lunabotics.
6. Teams must resolve all questions and rule clarifications before entering the MCC.
   The competition schedule will not be delayed accommodating last-minute requests.
7. MCJs are observers only and are not allowed to provide "help" during robot operations.
8. Mission Control and Arena judges have the authority to terminate a setup period or
   competition run at any time if the team is not following the rules.
9. Teams are expected to conduct themselves in a professional manner.
10. Teams are expected to use sound engineering practices and principles to operate their robot.
11. Team members must comply with all directions from the MCJ.
12. Disputes with MCJ direction or decisions must be elevated through the MCD.
13. Violation of the intent of a rule is a violation of the rule itself.

2. Mission Setup

1. Teams may not connect or interact with any equipment in the MCC until the setup period begins.
2. Setup period: Teams have up to 10 minutes to connect their laptops and routers,
   establish communications with their robot, and perform initial systems checkout.
3. During setup, the MCJ will provide the team with a handheld radio to communicate
   with team members in the Artemis Arena. Radio must be returned to the MCJ at the
   end of setup and may not be used during the competition run.
4. Arena team members are prohibited from pointing out obstacles, craters, and other
   arena conditions to the MCC team members.
5. Teams may use the situational awareness cameras during the setup period without penalty.

3. Mission Operations

1. Teams are allowed 30 minutes per competition run under nominal conditions.
2. Telerobotic operators will have access to two situational awareness cameras in the
   Artemis Arena via monitors provided in the MCC. The use of these cameras during
   the run will factor into the team's construction score.
3. Telerobotic operators are only allowed to use data and video originating from the robot
   and the competition video monitors.
4. It is the sole responsibility of team members in the MCC to communicate effectively
   with the MCJ to ensure every autonomy attempt is recognized and scored correctly.
   If the judge is not notified of the attempt in advance, the score will be zero points.
   Teams are expected to:
   - Clearly announce and make eye contact with the MCJ when going to autonomous operations
   - Clearly announce when autonomy has begun and has been completed each time
5. All autonomy attempts must be "Hands-Free," meaning no team members may touch
   any components (e.g., laptops, game controllers) until the team has declared
   autonomy completion or autonomy failure.
   - If a team member interacts with any equipment while the robot is still moving,
     the team will receive zero points for the attempt.
   - In the event of an autonomy failure, announce that the attempt has failed
     before resuming manual control.
   - Manipulation of the NASA situational awareness cameras is permitted during
     autonomy attempts.

4. Mission Anomalies

1. Once the competition timer has started, the robot has 5 minutes to move. If the robot
   has not moved by the 5-minute mark, the robot is considered inoperable and the run ends.
2. The team should notify the MCJ if their robot experiences an unrecoverable issue:
   - Loss of Comm: Robot functional but can no longer communicate properly with MCC
   - Loss of Locomotion: Robot ceases movement for 5 minutes
   - Loss of Excavation: Robot can no longer acquire regolith per its design
   - Loss of Deposition: Robot can no longer offload regolith per its design
   - Loss of Robot: Robot fully unable to execute the mission
3. In the event of a mission-ending anomaly that the team does not voluntarily end,
   Mission Control and Artemis Arena judges have the authority to terminate the attempt.
4. It is the team's responsibility to ensure they are executing corrective actions
   efficiently and communicating properly with the MCJ.

5. Mission Conclusion

1. Teams must cease operations when the competition timer ends. If the robot is in the
   middle of an autonomous activity, teams must send a command to inhibit further actions.
   Regolith offloading is permitted to be completed only if the robot was actively dumping
   material prior to the expiration of the competition timer.
2. Teams may not disconnect communications or begin dismantling MCC equipment
   until directed by the MCJ.
3. Teams should remain in the MCC until dismissed by the MCJ.
4. Teams are responsible for ensuring they leave the MCC with all equipment they brought.

KSC Communications Operations:

1. KSC Finals General Communication System Requirements

1. All teams shall use IEEE 802.11 wireless protocol standards for their wireless connection.
2. Routers are required to support both the 2.4 and 5 GHz bands.
3. Routers are required to have the ability to turn off the 2.4 GHz band.
4. Each team will be assigned an SSID upon checking in that must be used for their wireless equipment. Team's SSID will be written as "Team_##."
5. Teams are required to broadcast their SSID.
6. Robots and access points are ONLY allowed to transmit using their designated SSID.
7. Hidden networks are not allowed.
8. Wireless encryption is required.
9. Teams cannot use channel bonding for 2.4 GHz data transmission. Maximum spectral bandwidth setting of 20MHz for all wireless transmission equipment.
10. All team-provided wireless equipment shall operate legally within FCC requirements for unlicensed wireless equipment in the ISM radio frequency band.
11. Power amplification devices are NOT allowed in the competition.
12. Radio frequency power requirements apply to all wireless transmission devices at any ISM frequency.
13. Bluetooth transmission in the 2.4 GHz range is allowed for sensors and other robot communications at Classes 2 and 3 only (max 2.5 mW EIRP). Class 1 Bluetooth devices are not allowed.
14. 2.4 GHz Zigbee/IEEE 802.15.4 technology is prohibited.
15. Technology using other ISM non-licensed radio frequencies (900 MHz or 5 GHz) is allowed for robot or sensor systems but will not be monitored.
16. Teams can elect to use 5 GHz WiFi during competition but this frequency will not be monitored for wireless interference and will not be grounds for a protest or rematch.
17. All robots should have an external WiFi antenna.
18. Use of the NASA-provided situational awareness camera will add 500 Kbps of data use per camera.
19. The communications link is required to have an average data utilization bandwidth of no more than 4,000 Kbps.

2. KSC Finals General Radio Frequency and Communications Approval (Comm Check)

1. All teams are required to successfully pass a Comm Check prior to entering competition.
2. Compliance with all rules in Section 1 will be verified during the Comm Check.
3. Each team must demonstrate that their robot and access point are operating only on the assigned channel. Maximum 15 minutes at the communication judges' station.
4. Comm checks are to be performed on Channel 1.
5. Teams in the queue for a comm check may quickly configure to Channel 1, then turn their wireless off until at the comm test station.
6. Teams must identify and show to the judges all wireless emission equipment on the robot.
7. If the robot is transmitting low power Bluetooth or any non-2.4 or 5 GHz frequency equipment, printed documentation from the manufacturer must be provided to the judges.
8. If teams are using Bluetooth, printed documentation of the hardware in use must be provided.
9. Team must verify they are connected to the robot by remote wireless control.
10. Team must show they can turn off 2.4 GHz after demonstrating control.
11. If a team cannot demonstrate the above tasks in the allotted time, the team will be disqualified.

3. KSC Comm Rule Qualifications

Each team is required to command and monitor their robot over the NASA provided Artemis network infrastructure.

4. KSC RoboPits Comm Rules

1. All routers in the RoboPits shall turn off 2.4 GHz by default or power down their wireless equipment.
2. The RoboPits will have full access to the 5 GHz band and it is the required WiFi frequency band.
3. Teams may test their robot using 2.4 GHz with prior authorization from the Pit Boss, only on Channel 11, for a short period of time.
4. Teams in the queue for a comm check may quickly configure to Channel 1, then turn their router off until at the comm test station.
5. The RoboPits communication environment will be monitored:
   a. 1st time: warning and announcement to all teams
   b. 2nd time: 500 point deduction from final score
   c. 3rd time: disqualification from competition

5. The KSC Artemis Arena Comm Rules

1. Robot operations will be performed only on Channel 1.
2. Teams must be able to configure to Channel 1 within 15 minutes of being notified.
3. If a team is on the wrong channel during their competition attempts, the team will be disqualified.
4. Channel 1 will be monitored during competition.
5. Situational cameras are staged in the Artemis Arena. Joystick and display will be located with the team in the MCC.

6. KSC Communications [(Artemis Arena – Mission Control Center (MCC)]

1. Handheld radios will be provided to each team in the Artemis Arena during setup.
2. Each team will provide the wireless link (access point, bridge, or wireless device) to their robot.
3. Teams must set their own network IP addresses.
4. In the arena, Artemis will provide an elevated network drop (male RJ-45 Ethernet plug) that extends to the Mission Control Center.
5. Network connections to the robot are ONLY allowed via the WAP router and the cable going to the MCC. Any backchannel wireless connections are expressly forbidden and will result in disqualification.
6. A shelf will be set up next to the network drop at a height 0 to 0.5 meter above the walls, placed in a corner area. This is where you will place the Wireless Access Point (WAP).
7. KSC Artemis will provide a standard US NEMA 5-15 type, 110 VAC, 60 Hz electrical jack by the network drop.
8. Teams are strongly encouraged to develop a dust protection cover for their WAP.
9. During setup, teams will set up their access point and verify communication with their robot.

Autonomous Operations:

During each competition attempt, the team will earn up to 600 Construction points for
autonomous operation. The Caterpillar Autonomy Award will be based on the sum of
autonomy scores from both UCF runs and runs at KSC.

General Rules:
- "Hands-free" means all team members in mission control must not engage any components.
- Teams must announce the start and completion of every autonomy point attempt.
- If your autonomy attempt has failed, announce failure before beginning manual control.

Construction points for autonomous activities:

1. Excavation Automation: 75 pts
   1. The Excavation Zone overlaps with the Starting Zone. Teams may excavate anywhere in the Excavation Zone.
   2. Team must indicate to the MCJ that they are going hands-free for the excavation attempt.
   3. The robot must execute machine control commands itself during the excavation task.
   4. A discernable amount of regolith must be collected for transport. Hands-free operation must begin before the robot engages the regolith.
   5. Excavation mechanisms must be completely removed from contact with the regolith before returning to remote control.
   6. Once excavation is complete, team must indicate they are going to remote control.

2. Dump Automation - Full Run: 50 pts
   1. Teams are allowed to traverse the Obstacle Zone via remote control.
   2. Prior to crossing into the Construction zone, they must indicate to MCJ they are going hands-free.
   3. The robot must execute machine control commands itself during the dump task.
   4. A discernable amount of regolith must be placed at the berm location.
   5. Once dumping is complete, team must indicate they are going to remote control.

3. Travel Automation: 250 pts
   Teams must indicate to the MCJ they are going hands-free while still in the excavation zone.
   The robot must remain hands-free while crossing the obstacle field and crossing into the construction zone.
   Requirements:
   1. Localization across the entire competition arena
   2. Object detection and location relative to the robot
   3. Navigational planning based on location and obstacles
   4. The obstacle field will be constructed to require obstacle detection, mapping, and navigation planning.
      Teams shall NOT architect a "Point and traverse" approach.
   5. If the robot contacts a rock or drives across a crater, a 30-point reduction will be applied (one-time penalty).
   6. For maximum points, the attempt must be made at the start of the run on the first time leaving the excavation zone.
      A penalty of 50 points will be applied to any attempt that occurs after traversing the obstacle zone in remote control.

4. Full Autonomy (One Cycle): 450 pts
   1. Robot must be in hands-free control for one entire cycle.
   2. Attempt must be made at the beginning of the competition run.
   3. Teams may begin in remote control and move the robot within the starting zone only to localize.
      Teams must begin hands-free from the starting area and remain hands-free while:
      - Excavating
      - Crossing the obstacle field
      - Crossing into the construction zone
      - Dumping the regolith for the berm
      - Crossing the obstacle field and returning to the excavation zone
   4. If the robot comes in contact with a rock or drives across a crater, a 30-point reduction per occurrence,
      up to maximum of 90 points. The robot is allowed to move rocks and fill in craters in the excavation zone.

5. Full Autonomy: 600 pts
   1. The attempt must be made from the beginning of the competition run.
   2. The robot must be in hands-free control for the entire competition run.
   3. At least two complete cycles must be completed. Berm construction points must be achieved.
   4. If the robot comes in contact with a rock or drives across a crater, a 30-point reduction per occurrence,
      up to maximum of 90 points. Robot is allowed to move rocks and fill in craters in the excavation zone.

Allowable Combinations:
Ex: 1 - Excavation only:              75 pts
Ex: 2 - Dump only:                    50 pts
Ex: 3 - Travel only:                 250 pts
Ex: 4 - Excavation + Dump:           125 pts
Ex: 5 - Dump + Travel:               300 pts
Ex: 6 - Excavation + Dump + Travel:  375 pts
Ex: 7 - Full Autonomy (One Cycle):   450 pts
Ex: 8 - Full Autonomy:               600 pts

Note: Excavation, Dump, Travel automation points will NOT be combined with Full
Autonomy (One Cycle) or Full Autonomy.

GLOSSARY OF TERMS
================================================================================

1. Arena: Located in the Astronaut Memorial Foundation's Center for Space Education (CSE)
   where the robots will perform each competition attempt.

2. Astronaut Memorial Foundation's Center for Space Education (CSE):
   Located adjacent to the northwest end of the Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex (KSCVC),
   at the Eastern terminus of Florida S.R. 405 in building M6-306.
   https://www.amfcse.org/about-cse

3. Autonomous: The operation of a robot with no human interaction.

4. Basaltic Regolith Properties: This competition will assume that basaltic regolith properties
   are similar to the Lunar regolith as stated in the "Lunar Sourcebook: A User's Guide to the
   Moon", edited by G. H. Heiken, D. T. Vaniman, and B. M. French, copyright 1991,
   Cambridge University Press. https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar_sourcebook/

5. Black Point-1 (BP-1) (https://ares.jsc.nasa.gov/projects/simulants/bp-1.html):
   - It does not behave like sand.
   - The coefficient of friction has not been measured.
   - There are naturally occurring rocks in the aggregate.
   - BP-1 is made from crushed basalt fines and not commercially available.
   - The density of the compacted BP-1 aggregate will be between 1.5 g/cm3 and 1.8 g/cm3.
   - BP-1 behaves like a silty powder soil with most particles under 100 microns in diameter.
   - Will be compacted; the top layer will be raked to a fluffy condition of approximately
     0.75 g/cm3, similar to the Lunar surface.
   - Teams are encouraged to develop or procure simulants based on basaltic minerals
     and lunar surface regolith particle size, shape, and distribution.

6. Black Point-1 (BP-1) Reflectivity: NASA performed tests to answer questions about BP-1
   reflectivity for LIDAR (or other LASER-based) navigation systems. The laser is spread out
   as a sheet oriented in the vertical direction. The three images are for three angles of the laser.
   - Note the BP-1 is mounded so teams need to account for the fact that it is not a flat surface.
   - Three pictures were shot with the camera at 10, 16, and 21 degrees relative to the surface.
   - The laser was at an angle of 15 degrees. Camera settings (manual mode): 1/8 s, f/4.5.

7. Bot Shop: The Prototype Development Laboratory's (PDL) Bot Shop is a "mobile machine shop"
   with grinding, sanding, mini-mill and mini-lathe, band saw, drill press, and hand tools.
   No welding capability. Can help teams repair broken robots but cannot finish a started robot.
   https://public.ksc.nasa.gov/partnerships/capabilities-and-testing/testing-and-labs/prototype-development-laboratory/

8. Center for Lunar & Asteroid Surface Science (CLASS): At the intersection of NASA science
   and exploration for rocky, atmosphereless bodies.
   https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/

9. Exolith Lab® (https://exolithsimulants.com/): Enables space development and growth by
   providing high-fidelity analogs to researchers and educators globally.

10. In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) (https://beta.nasa.gov/mission/in-situ-resource-utilization-isru/):
    To live and work in deep space for months or years may mean crew members have less
    immediate access to life-sustaining elements. The farther humans go into deep space, the
    more important it will be to generate products with local materials.

11. Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex (KSCVC) (https://www.kennedyspacecenter.com/):
    A Smithsonian Affiliate offering exhibits and attractions that tell the NASA story, with
    artifacts from Mercury, Gemini, Apollo and Space Shuttle Programs.

12. Lunar Highlands Simulant (LHS-1): Developed by the CLASS Exolith Lab. A high-fidelity,
    mineral-based simulant appropriate for a generic or average highlands location on the Moon.
    https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/simulant_lunarhighlands/

13. Lunar Regolith Density: The density of regolith at the Apollo 15 landing site averages
    approximately 1.35 g/cm3 for the top 30 cm, and approximately 1.85 g/cm3 at a depth of
    60 cm. About half the weight of lunar soil is less than 60 to 80 microns in size.

14. Mission Control: Operations area where teams will operate or autonomously control their
    robotic excavator. Located outside of the Artemis Arenas.

15. NASA Application Website:
    https://stemgateway.nasa.gov/s/courseoffering/a0BSJ000003pitJ/lunabotics-2026
    The Team Lead starts the team application process and then invites students and faculty
    advisor(s) to apply. For issues on the NASA Gateway OSTEM application website, send
    inquiries to the website Help Desk.

16. Regolith Construction Robot: An autonomous or tele-operated robotic excavator including
    mechanical and electrical equipment, batteries, gases, fluids, and consumables delivered by
    a team to compete in the competition.

17. Regolith Construction Points: Points earned from the competition attempt will be used to
    determine ranking in the on-site robotic operations category.

18. Reference Point: A fixed location signified by an arrow showing the forward direction on the
    mining robot that will serve to verify the starting orientation of the mining robot within the mining arena.

19. RoboPits: Equipped with emergency eyewash stations and disposal containers for industrial
    waste. Teams are advised to bring additional LED lighting, power strips, and first-aid kits.
    This is where teams will work on their robots, meet other competitors, and have their robot
    inspected before it goes to work.

20. Rock/Gravel: The gravel is #57 limestone gravel (~2 cm in diameter) intended to simulate
    the icy-regolith buried in the South Polar region of the Moon. May be mixed in with BP-1.

21. Swamp Works: A lean-development, rapid innovation environment at NASA's Kennedy Space Center.
    Founded in 2012 when four laboratories in the Surface Systems Office were merged.
    https://public.ksc.nasa.gov/partnerships/capabilities-and-testing/technical-capabilities/swamp-works/

22. Telerobotic: The area of robotics concerned with the control of semi-autonomous robots
    from a distance, using television, wireless networks (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Deep Space Network)
    or tethered connections. Communication with and control of the mining robot during each
    competition attempt must be performed solely through the provided communications link,
    which is required to have a total average bandwidth of no more than 5.0 megabits/second
    on all data and video sent to and received from the mining robot.

23. University of Central Florida (UCF) (https://www.ucf.edu/): A public research university
    with its main campus in unincorporated Orange County, Florida. Part of the State University
    System of Florida.
